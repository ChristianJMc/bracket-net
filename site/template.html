How the forecast works

The picks on this site come from a linear model trained on point margin. It predicts how many points team A should win by, and turns that into a win chance using how much real games vary around such a prediction (about 11 points). A 25-point win teaches it more than a 1-point win, which is why it beat a neural network trained on wins and losses.

For each team, the pipeline rebuilds a season profile from raw box scores. It adjusts offense, defense and tempo for opponent strength and for home court, including the extra edge altitude programs get at home. Then it adds the four factors (shooting, turnovers, rebounding, free throws), shot mix, schedule strength, recent form and volatility. Profiles are frozen on Selection Sunday, and the model never sees seeds.

Nine kinds of model were tested the same way: each season predicted using only what was known before its tournament, with nothing from that tournament or any later one. The adopted model is the simplest one whose results are within one standard error of the best.

Sure things and coin flips

What decided the games

What the data says

  • Simpler beats bigger. Larger neural networks fit their training games better and did worse on new seasons. Boosted trees were the best model in some years and the worst in others. Averaging several different simple models helped most.
  • Altitude is real. Home teams at high elevation (BYU, Utah St., Colorado, New Mexico) win by more at home than other teams do, which used to inflate their ratings. The ratings now account for it.
  • Size travels. Effective height is the input that most often earns a place beyond the base ratings.
  • Most situational factors don't show up. Rest days, travel, tip-off time on a team's body clock, crowd, home uniforms, earlier meetings, coach experience and pace all came out within noise across 937 games.
  • Facts outweigh opinions. Inputs counted from games and ratings built on them carry nearly all the weight. The one opinion that earns a small place is the AP preseason poll.
  • Seeds are the weakest signal. Every rating-based model beats the committee's seed list in every resampling of the seasons.

Architecture

Each network is a small multilayer perceptron with tanh units. It scores the matchup from A's side, g(x), and from B's side, g(−x). The win probability is σ(g(x) − g(−x)), so P(A beats B) and P(B beats A) always add to exactly 100%. Five networks with different random starts are averaged.

What it doesn't know

  • Injuries announced after Selection Sunday. A box-score signal (whether a team's top players played their usual minutes in its last three games) was tested and didn't clear the bar.
  • How hot a team will shoot. Three-point luck decides a large share of upsets, and no pregame number predicts it.
  • Anything about the specific day: tested factors such as rest, travel and tip-off time added nothing measurable.

About 30% of tournament games go against any sensible favorite. The goal is to rate each game's risk correctly, which the table of sure things and coin flips checks.

Data

  • Box scores for every Division I game, 2008–2026 (2020 had no tournament): Barttorvik season files.
  • Brackets, seeds, and results: Wikipedia's tournament pages, cross-checked game by game against the box scores.
  • Ratings are computed here from those box scores, not copied from any ratings site.
  • Tested alongside them: KenPom, Barttorvik, EvanMiya, Heat Check and AP poll data (Kaggle, CC0), ESPN player box scores, venue and tip-off details, Sports-Reference coach records, and Open-Meteo elevation and weather.
  • Betting lines and public pick percentages are never used.

The 2027 field is announced on Selection Sunday in March 2027. Re-running the three build scripts adds it.

Bracket Net is an independent model and is not affiliated with the NCAA. Probabilities describe uncertainty; they are not guarantees. Not betting advice.

How AI is used. The forecasts come from statistical models trained on past box scores, not from a generative AI chatbot. A separate step can use Claude (Anthropic) to turn public game coverage into structured notes, such as who was injured. That step runs offline, only when the site's owner starts it, and its inputs are off by default, so it doesn't affect the forecasts shown here.
